VLSI – 8-bit Ripple Carry Adder

CMOS transistor-level implementation, layout optimization, and post-layout timing analysis.

Project Overview

Designed and implemented an 8-bit ripple-carry adder at the CMOS transistor level, including physical layout, routing optimization, and post-layout timing analysis. The project emphasized modular architecture, layout symmetry, and propagation delay evaluation.

Tools: Microwind, LTspice

Domain: CMOS VLSI, Layout, Timing

Focus: Modularity, Symmetry, Delay

Planning and Collaboration

The project followed a staged engineering workflow that separated architectural design, schematic validation, and physical implementation. We first defined and verified a transistor-level 1-bit full adder in LTSpice before scaling the design into an 8-bit ripple-carry architecture. This approach ensured logical correctness and timing behavior were validated prior to committing to physical layout in Microwind.

Engineering Workflow

  • Architecture definition: designed a modular 1-bit full adder cell as the scalable building block
  • Schematic validation: implemented and simulated the design in LTSpice to confirm logic correctness and baseline timing
  • Physical implementation: translated the schematic into transistor-level CMOS layout in Microwind
  • Optimization loop: iterated routing, symmetry, and layout organization to reduce parasitics and congestion
  • Post-layout verification: simulated the final layout to analyze propagation delay and carry-chain behavior

Architecture

The design is built around a modular 1-bit CMOS full adder cell that computes Sum = A ⊕ B ⊕ Cin and Cout = AB + (A ⊕ B)Cin. Each cell is implemented at the transistor level using complementary pull-up (PMOS) and pull-down (NMOS) networks. Eight identical cells are cascaded to form a ripple-carry architecture, where the carry-out of each stage feeds the next stage's carry-in. In the worst case, the carry must propagate through all stages, producing a delay that scales approximately linearly with bit width.

Design Theory (LTSpice)

  • Logic implementation: Boolean expressions rewritten using NAND-based equivalents (DeMorgan transformations) for efficient CMOS realization.
  • 1-bit cell: transistor-level CMOS implementation producing Sum and Carry outputs from inputs A, B, and Cin.
  • Scaling strategy: eight identical cells cascaded (Cout,i → Cin,i+1) to form the ripple-carry adder.
  • Timing model: worst-case propagation delay dominated by the carry chain where tpd,total ≈ N · tcarry.

1-bit Full Adder Building Block

  • Inputs: A, B, Cin
  • Outputs: Sum, Cout
  • CMOS pull-up/pull-down transistor networks

8-bit Ripple-Carry Integration

  • Cout of each stage drives the next stage’s Cin
  • Modular structure enables straightforward scaling
  • Critical path dominated by carry propagation

Layout Strategy (Microwind)

Design Goals

  • Preserve layout symmetry to maintain consistent electrical behavior across stages
  • Minimize interconnect length to reduce parasitic resistance and capacitance
  • Maintain clean and repeatable VDD/GND distribution
  • Enable modular tiling of the 1-bit cell for scalable integration

Implementation Decisions

  • Standardized a compact 1-bit layout that tiles cleanly across 8 stages
  • Routed the carry path deliberately since it dominates the critical delay
  • Maintained consistent transistor orientation to simplify routing
  • Used Metal2 strategically for longer interconnects and crossings

The modular bit-slice layout allows the architecture to scale beyond 8 bits with minimal structural modification, preserving routing consistency and timing behavior.

Routing Optimization and Iteration

The physical layout was refined through several iterations to improve routing clarity and reduce delay along the critical carry path. Early layout versions exposed areas of routing congestion and longer interconnect runs, which increased parasitic resistance and capacitance. The design was progressively reorganized to shorten critical signal paths, maintain symmetry between bit-slices, and improve overall layout regularity.

What I optimized

  • Reduced routing congestion in regions with multiple signal crossings
  • Shortened carry-chain interconnects, which dominate worst-case propagation delay
  • Improved bit-slice symmetry to maintain consistent parasitic behavior across stages
  • Standardized routing patterns for easier verification and debugging

How I validated improvements

  • Re-ran Microwind post-layout simulation after major layout revisions
  • Compared propagation delay across design iterations
  • Verified logical correctness under multiple input switching scenarios
  • Monitored timing consistency along the carry propagation path

Post-Layout Simulation and Timing Analysis

After completing the physical layout, I ran post-layout simulations in Microwind to evaluate timing behavior with real interconnect parasitics. Unlike schematic-level simulation, this stage captures the impact of wire length, routing capacitance, and transistor placement on propagation delay.

What I measured

  • Input-to-output propagation delay along the critical carry chain
  • Timing consistency of Sum and Carry outputs across all 8 stages
  • Effects of routing parasitics on switching behavior

Test approach

  • Applied synchronized pulse inputs to A, B, and Cin to trigger carry propagation
  • Observed output transitions across bit positions to identify delay accumulation
  • Compared results against the LTSpice schematic-level timing baseline
  • Identified the carry chain as the dominant contributor to worst-case delay

As expected for ripple-carry architectures, propagation delay increased across stages as the carry signal propagated through the chain. The post-layout results confirmed that interconnect parasitics introduce additional delay compared to ideal schematic simulation.

Key Takeaways

  • Physical layout decisions directly impact timing through parasitics and interconnect length.
  • Modular cell design makes scaling (1-bit → 8-bit) cleaner and easier to verify.
  • Optimization is an iterative loop: route → simulate → revise → re-simulate.
